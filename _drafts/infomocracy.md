What if Google, Ground News, Palantir, Flock, Meta, and ABC/Disney all merged into a single company, and that company was then charged with facilitating all global elections, entertainment, and telecommunications? To any reasonable person in /[current year/], this idea sounds unambiguously dystopian and something that everyone in the world, regardless of nationality or political affiliation, would agree should be rejected out of hand. But that is precisely what the people of the world of Infomocracy have apparently signed up for. And I would say that Infomocracy ends up being another iteration of "Do Not Build The Torment Nexus: A Cautionary Tale", but most of the characters in the novel (and perhaps the author) adopt an ambivalent attitude toward this arrangement even by the end of the story. Except the main character/author self-insert. Maybe.

When I first discovered Infomocracy, it immediately shot to the top of my fiction TBR list. The premise of this novel ticks all the boxes of something I would be interested in and should love: post-cyberpunk, political thriller, femme protagonist written by a woman, speculation about alternative government and electoral systems. I made it my mission to hunt down physical copies of this entire series (Null States and State Tectonics are the sequels) and was fortunate to find all three at my local 2nd & Charles in very good condition for ~$6 a book. I had interacted with the author, Malka Older, on Twitter and BlueSky a few times and she struck me as friendly, very intelligent and knowledgeable about conflict and global emergencies, and a haver of good opinions; not nearly the big, sad doomer #Resistance lib her science fiction forebearers (William Gibson) acts like on social media. So all of that is to say, I was very excited to read this book and optimistic that it'd be the first of a new paradigm of post-cyberpunk, or cyberprep, fiction.

I had set my expectations way too high for a debut novel written by an academic whose area of expertise wasn't creative writing, is what I later discovered. As a reader, various aspects of the prose, some half-baked world building, and an abrupt end that left some loose threads and tied up others with minimal sense of resolution left me wishing that a more experienced novelist had tried to tackle this novel's premise (I do have Neal Stephenson's Interface on my TBR, but Cryptonomicon was a massive turn-off so it's low on the list).

The prose, to be blunt, made me feel like I was reading a novel written for young adults.

# Liberty aggression built up throughout the book and then never carried through, abruptly defused

# hints that Information is behind everything but then not fleshed out

# Heritage complicity in earthquake and election interference solved with minimal fanfare

# no explanation for how a new election was called, approved by voters, and smoothed over

# minimal explanation about the Supermajority and what additional powers it confers to a government

I am told that the subsequent novels in this series see Older's writing and plotting improve, so I intend to read them and write an overall series review once finished. But Infomocracy does not stand on its own, and it brings me no pleasure to report as much.
