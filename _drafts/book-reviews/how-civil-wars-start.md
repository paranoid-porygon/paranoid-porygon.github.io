"How Civil Wars Start, and How to Stop Them" is nonfiction for a popular audience written by political scientist and relevant subject matter expert Barbara F. Walter. It's a pretty strong candidate for "this book could have been an article," but in this case doesn't mean it isn't worth your time. My only major gripe is that in the last chapter about how to actually prevent civil wars, she gives a lot of nondescript policy proposals (specifically regarding regulation of social media) that I wish had been more specific; it's easy to predict how those kinds of laws would be challenged in court, and I'd like to see more robust recommendations. Otherwise, the content about the necessary conditions for civil war to break out is worth picking up the book if you aren't a freak who already has a non-US passport and/or stockpile of trauma first aid stuff shoved in every backpack, purse, and bag you own.

I actually first heard about this book when YouTube recommended me an interview with the author, and I then saw it at Barnes&Noble very shortly thereafter. Maybe this was blitz marketing and maybe I got incepted by social media into making this purchase, but the content matter is absolutely in my wheelhouse (i.e. conflict and extremism in the social media age) and I probably would have purchased it anyways.



## Summary

The book is 8 chapters, and each chapter about a necessary condition for civil war features a case study (or multiple) that illustrate the phenomenon the chapter is about.

### Conditions for civil war

#### Anocracy

The interstitial zone between democracy and autocracy is known as *anocracy*, and existing there is one of the most informative predictors that a country will descend into civil war. The author discusses how political scientists use a metric called the Polity Score that quantifies how autocratic or democratic a country is on a scale from -10 to +10, respectively; anocracies exist in the -5 to +5 zone. She gives some examples on what factors are considered in assigning a Polity Score to a given country, but she doesn't go into how exactly it's tabulated (I'm sure this is something you can look up).

The case example for this chapter is Iraq after the toppling of Saddam Hussein and how the US's attempt to rapidly democratize the country shot it into the anocracy zone, which did lead to a civil war.

#### Factionalism 

Not to be confused with polarization, *factionalism* is when a country ends up divided according to immutable characteristics --usually ethnicity or religion-- which creates defined groups of belligerents to participate in a civil war. The author quickly dispells the idea that ethnically diverse countries are more prone to conflict than ethnically homogeneous ones are simply by nature of being diverse (a common talking point for white nationalists); instead, what needed to be seen were political divisions along ethnic or religious lines, if political parties tacked to race or religion, and if when one party assumed power if it tried to rule at the expense or exclusion of others. Anocracy and factionalism were together the best predictors of where a civil war was likely to break out.

Post-Soviet Yugoslavia is the case study for this chapter, which is apt because the conflicts that came out of that region gave us the term "ethnic cleansing". Rather than coalescing around political ideologies after Tito died, Yugoslavia was persuaded by *ethnic entrepreneurs* to fracture along ethnic lines.

The author also highlights the risk of *superfactions*, where a faction shares multiple attributes: race, ethnicity, religion, class, and geography. A country that sees a superfaction develop is twelve times more likely to see a civil war.

Countries that are divided into two dominant groups are even more likely to see civil war.

#### Downgraded group status

Many ethnic groups live in factionalized societies but never kick off a civil war. Those that do usually do so because they have or had a majority or political hegemony but detect (or merely believe) that their power is slipping away. The author refers to this as *downgrading*, and she says it is a reliable way to predict who will start the conflict in a country primed for civil war.

The example for this condition is the post-WWII Philippines.

#### Loss of hope


The author cites the failure of Irish Catholics' peaceful protests and petitioning and the subsequent Battle of the Bogside where British MPs turned on Irish civillians, which led to a total loss of hope for a peaceful solution and the beginning of The Troubles.
 
### The author really hates social media, and she's right

There is no better case study for why social media companies are profoundly evil than the Rohingya Genocide in Myanmar, and that is exactly the example the author uses.

### We're pretty fucking close

### Years of Lead 2: Electric Boogaloo

The author starts the 7th chapter with a speculative account of what organized civil conflict in the US could look like. In short, it's the Years of Lead that Italy went through: asymmetric attacks on public infrastructure, civilian population centers where "undesirables" live or work, targeted attacks on politicians or public figures, and disinformation campaigns executed under the fog of war.
