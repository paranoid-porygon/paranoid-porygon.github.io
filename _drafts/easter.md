A holistic reading of the canon gospels paints a cohesive picture of the morality of Jesus: pursuing the maximum punishment available for every wrong committed is not only unproductive and barbarous when widely applied, it is ungodly, therefore the world would be better if more people practiced radical compassion, meekness, deference, modesty, generosity, and forgiveness.

This moral framework is explicated repeatedly through Jesus's parables. The Parable of the Good Samaritan invited his peers to interrogate their own racism and reevaluate what it means to be neighborly (and that neighborliness is good). The Parables of the Prodigal Son and of the Workers in the Vineyard extoled the virtues of generosity and criticized those who demand "just desserts". When Jesus said "the Kingdom of Heaven is like this" before reciting a parable, he was laying out a blueprint for how societies on Earth could replicate heaven.

Ultimately Jesus was a victim of the exact thing that he preached against: a barbarously violent state and religious order that sought the maximum available punishment (crucifixion) for a bogus crime. The Roman state executed people for petty theft while employing pedophiles as centurions. The Hellenistic Jewish leaders were, according to the gospels, more concerned with superficial piety than the underlying logic of those scriptural commands: the exercise of charity, hospitality, and compassion. Obsession with the pursuit of the fullest extent of the law, especially when the punishment is disproportionate to the crime, is definitionally anti-Christian.

Two thousand years after Jesus's life, the world has come a long way since the comparatively primitive and morally rotten Roman Empire, and much of that progress can be traced back to the moral teachings of Jesus. You don't get liberal democracy without the American Revolution. You don't get the American Revolution without the Enlightenment. You don't get the Enlightenment without the Protestant Reformation. And you don't get the Protestant Reformation without Jesus. The understanding that every human is created in the image of God and therefore deserves dignity is the foundational virtue for each of these movements. Protestants believed that everyone had the ability to read and understand scripture without needing a clergy as intermediary. The Enlightenment thinkers likewise believed that humans could observe and understand the world and ascertain truth without needing dogma. The American Founders extrapolated this and applied it to a skepticism of kings and monarchies and founded a liberal republic on the idea that humans can make their own decisions.
