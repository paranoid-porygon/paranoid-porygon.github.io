Today is Holocaust Remembrance Day.

In a 1945 letter, Dwight Eisenhower who at the time led the Allied forces in Europe said this regarding what he and other Allied soldiers witnessed:

"The visual evidence and the verbal testimony of starvation, cruelty and bestiality were so overpowering as to leave me a bit sick. In one room, where they [there] were piled up twenty or thirty naked men, killed by starvation, George Patton would not even enter. He said he would get sick if he did so. I made the visit deliberately, in order to be in position to give first-hand evidence of these things if ever, in the future, there develops a tendency to charge these allegations merely to 'propaganda'."

Eisenhower made it a point to document everything that they found with cameras and written records. 

The Nuremberg trials where the perpetrators of the Holocaust were tried, convicted (or not), and sentenced served not merely as a vehicle for justice but as an additional, indisputable historical record of what had happened. The perpetrators were offered the same access to legal defense that criminal defendants were offered in the United States, and judges tried to be as lenient as both reasonable and possible so that there could be no claim that these were merely show trials to provide cover for victims seeking revenge; the same could not be said of the concurrent trials held by the Eastern Bloc. One hundred and ninety-nine people were tried, 161 were convicted, and only 37 were sentenced to death. But all of their crimes and respective levels of complicity were recorded for posterity to remember.

Less than 20 years after the end of the war, Germans who were too young to remember its events openly disputed the recorded facts and questioned the authenticity of documents such as Anne Frank's diary. A series of events that included the outing of a German Chancellor as a former Nazi, the extraordinary rendition of Adolf Eichmann from Argentina to Israel by Mossad agents for trial and execution, and the forming of the Office of Special Investigations inside the United States to identify and deport war criminals who had lied about their alibis during the war to gain refugee status and later permanent residency or citizenship all helped to once again publicize the atrocities of the Holocaust for the next generation to see for themselves.

As time went on and we are more far removed from those events, the work of maintaining Holocaust memory has fallen upon museum curators, filmmakers and documentarians, writers for historical books, shows, and video games, and teachers. Despite the lengths to which witnesses and survivors went to document those events so that this would happen "Never Again", we today have extremists make the simultaneous contradictory claims that "there's no way that 6 million people could have been killed" and "6 million wasn't enough". We have popular children's authors denying the indisputable evidence that queer, particularly transgender, people were victims of the Holocaust, a belief that German courts have determined constitutes Holocaust denial. We see the leaders of a country populated by the descendants of survivors and victims of the Holocaust replicating the atrocities of the Nazis. And we see the country that formerly led the Allies and the effort to document the Holocaust openly mimic the Third Reich's suppression of truthful events that we can witness with our own eyes.

Holocaust remembrace was only possible because of extensive records, photographs, films, and testimonies from witnesses and survivors.
