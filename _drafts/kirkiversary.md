Being polemic isn't by itself a logical fallacy. It's an intentional stylistic choice mean to bait out people who are going to take offense at its content. aka trolling. Most of my posts look like this, and they intentionally bait everyone from anti-gun democrats to right-wingers who don't think gender dysphoria is real.

Charlie Kirk's organization was funded in ways that obscure donation channels. (https://factually.co/fact-checks/politics/turning-point-usa-dark-money-4395f5) You can argue that this isn't bad on its face, but that context is necessary when talking about how his organization grew. I was involved in collegiate libertarian organizations at the time he entered the scene and tried to (back then) position himself as a right-libertarian, and it was shocking how quickly his org grew and his antics became well-known relative to other libertarian and youth orgs that had been doing the work longer, and how libertarian politics in the US shifted from "free markets, free people" to "we need to combat 'liberal indoctrination' in schools" up to and including discouraging young people from pursuing higher education (https://www.foxnews.com/politics/charlie-kirk-higher-education-scam-new-book-encourages-americans-anything-college). We eventually found out that was precisely because of his obscure financial backers. It was not surprising when he later made a total heel-turn away from trying to appear libertarian and did a full send on his national conservatism project, with increasing attention paid to his Christian identity. 

Rather than focusing on arguing for free market economics like the rest of us libertarians were doing, his focus became highlighting supposed "liberal indoctrination" in American universities, up to and including creating a public list of professors that he felt held objectionable views and having student members of TPUSA narc on suspected pinkos. It wasn't until this week that that project ended. (https://www.nbcwashington.com/investigations/turning-point-usa-professor-watchlist-instructors-impact/4151613/) It was McCarthyism and the anti-education campaign in all one effort. That doesn't strike me as "doing politics the right way," as Ezra Klein put it. It strikes me as disrupting classroom learning and creating witch hunts. If the right wants to come after the SPLC for publicly accusing individuals and organizations of being member of hate groups, they need to acknowledge that this was a very similar project, and with very similar potential negative outcomes (loss of jobs, targeted violence).

Speaking of violence, while Kirk never explicitly called for his listeners to commit violent acts (media personalities like this know better than to torpedo their career by removing all plausible deniability), there were a handful of times his rhetoric got a little too close for comfort. I think the most egregious example is the Paul Pelosi quip where he encouraged some "brave patriot" to try to bail out Pelosi's assailant; you can argue that if he were held on bail (he wasn't), there's nothing illegal about that, but it leaves me with the questions of 1) why the guy that attacked the husband of a politician you don't like, and 2) why not other perpetrators of violence who you can also bring on the show to interview as he suggested doing with Peolsi's assailant? You can't point at this and blow the logical fallacy whistle and flag me for whataboutism when this is precisely how folks like Kirk operate and intentionally structure their rhetoric so that their disingenuousness can't be called out; watch any of Kirk's debates and you'll see that whataboutism is his favorite rhetorical strategy. If any left-of-center pundit had similarly said something about the UHC CEO assassin, for instance, they should have got excoriated, but I don't remember hearing of any doing that; there were plenty of rando lefties online trying to claim the assailant as one of their own and fawning over the guy, but I don't recall seeing anyone near the level of Kirk's notoriety doing that, or even just suggesting that they pay his bail. Kirk's rhetoric didn't strictly encourage violence against liberal politicians, and it's fair to say that you can't hold someone to account for the actions of one of their deranged followers, but instances like the Pelosi bit severely muddy his "I'm just a guy asking questions" brand, and it stands in stark juxtaposition to the democratic politicians and pundits that came out in the wake of Kirk's death who made a big song and dance over condemning political violence out of fear of reprisal or copycats. Tiptoeing near the line is the name of the game for these podcaster pundit types, irrespective of their political affiliation. 

Speaking of juxtapositions: my comparison of Kirk to Fred Rogers. That quote from the young man in the article felt similar to if I said something like "I admire Lee Harvey Oswald for his marksmanship skills": I get what you are saying, but there are **clearly** better role models for the specific thing you admire this person for and who were arguably better at it, without all the  baggage that they are even more well-known for. Insofar as "what it looks like to hold unpopular social views with conviction", Fred Rogers filmed and aired the famous pool episode of his show while de facto segregation of places like public pools were still ongoing (https://mymodernmet.com/mister-rogers-pool-scene/). If you know of an instance where Kirk took a daring public stance that had as wide of a reach as Rogers did here, feel free to share; all I'm aware of Kirk having done was go from college-to-college to pick fights with teenagers over hackneyed talking points, which I imagine was validating to some students (like the one from the article) that held the same views as Kirk to see a rich guy in his 30s "fighting the good fight" in the same way that a black girl watching the Little Mermaid live-action remake feels validated.

Regarding "speak[ing] openly about saving faith in Jesus Christ," I think you and I both know, having grown up in Christian spaces, that this kind of talk is cheap. If all you do is publicly profess your faith in Christ and reiterate the need for others (or even "the nation") to convert, but that faith then doesn't translate into any newly meaningful action besides evangelism (and adherence to a few token rules that the general public doesn't already agree upon, most of which are conspicuously concerned with sex and gender), what kind of faith is that even? It's identitarianism, and too often I've seen the name of Christ used as a veneer to slather on top of flagrantly diabolical movements, groups, and projects. And that's why I brought up the Fred Rogers comparison: Rogers was an ordained Presbyterian minister whose project was pioneering the concept of childhood social-emotional development, and encouraging positive development in a way that was free and accessible. I would go so far as to say this was inspired by his ardent belief that every human being was created in the image of God and was deserving of the dignity of an education (especially if you are a Protestant who believes in "priesthood of all believers"), arguably the opposite of Kirk's anti-education campaign, which was inspired by his donors.

Points of comparisons are not red herrings. They are a method for highlighting how poorly conceived a thought was, and the idea that Kirk was "an example of what it looks like to hold unpopular social views with conviction and to speak openly about saving faith in Jesus Christ" is an indictment of whatever "social views" the person in the CT article holds and what their particular faith in Jesus looks like. They deserve better heroes.
