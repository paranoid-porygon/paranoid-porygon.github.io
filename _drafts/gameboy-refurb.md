A couple months ago, the YouTube recommender algorithm almost got me. I was fed a ton of videos about how "modding" a New Nintendo 3DS (N3DS) is the be-all-end-all handheld gaming console. Convinced of this argument, and not having purchased a N3DS when they were in production for no other reason that a mainline Pokemon game was never released exclusively on that console, I did cursory research on where to get one, found a color I liked, and let it sit in my shopping cart for 24 hours; I established this policy as a hard rule to prevent impulsive, psyop-driven purchases that I otherwise didn't plan on making. A *used* N3DS, despite being two console generations old, was more expensive than a brand new Switch OLED model, so I hesitated long past that initial 24 hours. Yeah, it would be cool to play Pokemon games from generations 1 through 7 on a single console, however 3 of those generations would be emulated and likely not able to transfer data to the official Bank system. It would be fun to try to get the thing to play games meant for PC or other consoles, but that wasn't a burning need in my life. Really the most compelling argument for getting the N3DS and modding it was to copy the ROMs and backup the saves for each of my physical DS and 3DS games, but I already had a 2DS that I could mod and achieve that. I resolved that this was ultimately a superfluous, luxury purchase and was not a priority. I might revist this in the future since the foldable N3DS XL is more portable than my 2DS, but I can make do in the meantime.

What caused me to entertain that impulsive almost-purchase for as long as I did was the desire to replay my old games on actual hardware, rather than emulators, without having to wipe the saves on my physical game cartridges. A friend of mine had an almost polar opposite need at around the same time: he wanted a way to quickly use GameShark cheats on Pokemon Silver version to make competitive teams for everyone to use on Pokemon Stadium 2, however doing this with a physical gameshark took much more time than doing so on an emulator. Luckily I was aware of a [usb-c enabled cartridge][1] that he could drop both a ROM and save file onto for exactly this purpose, for which I sent him the link and he ordered. Having received it in the mail months ago but never having the opportunity to test the cartridge, he handed it off to me to figure out. I went home, got out a dremel to make the usb-c port fit in the GameBoy cartrdige shell it came with, and loaded onto it my favorite romhack: [Pokemon Crystal Clear][2].

The cart worked on almost everything I tested it on: my childhood GameBoy Color, the GameBoy Player for the Gamecube that I bought myself freshman year of college back before pandemic-era influencers had inspired a run on retro consoles and drove up their prices, and the Gameboy Advanced that my younger brother abandoned in favor of his GBA SP; it mostly worked on Pokemon Stadium, however because that uses flawed software emulation, there were some game-breaking bugs when starting a New Game that forced me to move to the Gameboy Player. After creating a custom avatar and picking Porygon as my starter (this romhack absolutely rules), I played for some time before having to shut off the TV so my partner could try to fall asleep, at which point I pivoted back to my GameBoy Color.

I have owned this GameBoy for almost 27 years. It was well-taken care of but also extremely worn at the same time; the screen was in fair condition and the shell was intact even if a bit discolored from oily child hands holding it for hours on end, however the A, right, and start buttons all had difficulty registering when pressed from years of platforming and roleplaying games. I had also forgotten how difficult it was to see what was happening on the screen when not in direct sunlight and how desaturated the colors on the eponymous GameBoy Color were; this is great if you don't want to stare at a backlight before bed, but bad if you can't have an overhead light or lamp on while your partner sleeps. During the cursory research I did regarding modding a N3DS, I stumbled upon GameBoy modding and refurb/restoration, and given that playing this romhack on original hardware was the most enjoyable gaming experience I had had since maybe the release of Pokemon Alpha Sapphire/Omega Ruby, I had an easy time justifying the cost to give my own GameBoy Color a complete makeover in both form and function.

What follows is a writeup of what I did and what parts I used. There are a variety of vendors selling replacement parts, upgrades, and flashable cartridges, with some being better or more reputable than others. Hopefully this will save you time, money, and pain if you too decide to refurbish your old GameBoy...or one that you bought on eBay because you sold yours 20 years ago to trade up for a GameBoy Advanced.

# Modifying the Gameboy

One hard rule I established for this project was that no permanent modifications could be made to the motherboard. No soldering anything new onto the board, and no desoldering anything unless it was completely corroded or nonfunctional. This was a concern I had with the speaker before I got the thing opened up, but fortunately there was no apparent (or at least severe) corrosion on the speaker, and wiping it down as well as cleaning the volume potentiometer with a hefty amount of isopropyl alcohol mostly fixed the volume issues I noticed during pre-refurb testing. I was lucky enough to not have to solder anything new to the board, and it remains in factory condition 27 years later.
