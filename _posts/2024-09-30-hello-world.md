I have a visceral dislike for what the web has become. What originated as a way to share academic and government research quickly and persistently was eventually opened up to public use, and not long after that commercial interests began stumbling over themselves and each other as they rapidly tried to figure out the easiest ways to make a buck off the world wide web and the broader Internet. Those early dotcoms went after the low-hanging fruit of actually useful services that people actually wanted (think AOL, PayPal, eBay, Amazon). But as the web matured and all the most easily accessible entrepreneurial fruit had been picked, online capitalism did what all flavors of capitalism eventually do: shift from the laborious task of providing goods and services to the lackadaisical endeavor of rent-seeking. 

This shift began as a result of two specific simultaneous technological developments: ubiquitous computing, which became mainstream with the release of the iPhone in 2007, and social media, which blew up when Facebook became available to the general public in 2006. Those two events saw the barrier to entry to using the web be completely torn down: the iPhone made it trivial to get online anytime, anywhere, and Facebook made it trivial to produce and, more importantly, consume content on the web. The volume, variety, and velocity of what was being produced and shared on the net went asymptotic. And as we all are now aware, that led to the new business of Big Data.

Why sell a product to your users when you can get the users to sign up to *be* the product? That's roughly the business model of every free service on the internet: surveil your users and then sell their data. We scroll on Facebook and click on a specific link, they track that behavior and then feed us ads about related products and sell our data to other advertisers and widget and doodad companies. Then came endless scroll, which coopted our brains' dopaminergic pathways and poor perception of the passage of time to keep us looking at algorithmically suggested content, mostly ads and cute animal videos and occasionally a life update from a friend (you know, the ostensible purpose of the damn site). And then TikTok cracked the code and perfected algorithmically suggested endless scroll by having it feed short-form video, which engages multiple sensory modalities while not requiring an overwhelming time commitment. It's like a Skinner Box that feeds you cocaine and then sells your vital signs to Pfizer and the police department.
