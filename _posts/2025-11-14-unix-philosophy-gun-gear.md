## The Unix Philosophy

If you too are [cursed with knowledge][rms-foot-food], you know what Unix is. If you are untainted by the original sin of being a computer user of a certain age, here's a very quick primer: Unix is a computer operating system developed in the late '60s and released in the early '70s that was designed to be general purpose and to be able to run on a variety of computer hardware rather than being vendor locked like much of IBM's software at the time. It enabled programmers to develop a variety of purpose-built tools that would eventually become standard computing utilities, encorporated into most modern operating systems but especially Mac OS X and led to the rise of the Free Software Movement, the GNU project, and ultimately the Linux and BSD operating systems. Computing, let alone the internet, as we know it today would not exist were it not for Unix, its architects, its reverse-engineers, and the design philosophy that it inspired.

The [Unix Philosophy][unix-philosophy] as codified in the 1978 Bell System Technical Journal specifically applies to the development of software tools, but attempts have been made to distill its core ethos into a general purpose philosophy of use. That distillation looks like the following points:

1) Make a thing that does one thing and does it well
2) Make things that work together
3) Make things that share a universal interface/standard
4) Make things easy to put together, test, and execute
5) Focus on economy and elegance of design due to constraints (monetary cost, weight, time, etc)
6) Make things that are self-supporting

### Case study: just let a phone be a phone

Over the past year, I have been attempting to apply this philosophy in divesting from my cellphone as a do-it-all device, with varying levels of success. I had already stopped accessing social media and entertainmnet on my phone by that point, so the changes that were left to attempt were a bit more drastic. I plan to eventually do a writeup of this experiment, but in summary: I attempted to pivot to pen-and-paper for note taking, calendar reminders, task tracking, and check logging, I tried to use a standalone GPS unit instead of Google Maps, I switched to GrapheneOS to de-Google my phone as much as was reasonable, I converted an old phone into a pseudo-iPod, I started using a Casio G-shock instead of a smart watch, I went on the hunt for [the perfect laptop][perfect-laptop] for portable general purpose computing, and I started self-hosting as many services as I had the time to spin up rather than keep paying for Google Drive.

This process was broadly successful but ultimately led me to some different alternatives besides the ones I had originally envisioned. The GPS unit was not as usable as an open source map app, a pocket calendar was not as private as Proton Calendar on my encrypted phone, and the old touchscreen phone was less safe to use as car audio device than an actual iPod with actual buttons. But the watch worked out perfectly, and I have much more privacy and control over the self-hosted and open souce alternative services I got set up. TBD on the laptop as I struggle to source a battery replacement that doesn't cost me $90.

Overall, I was pleased with the results of this project. I've seen a variety of vloggers attempt something similar, sometimes as a way to divest from cellphone usage and others trying to improve their EDC and day-to-day workflows. 

### Can this be applied to gear?

Speaking of vloggers and YouTubers, I was fed a video by Dirty Civilian ostensibly arguing against kit proliferation and in favor of gear stacking/layering, i.e. having a slick plate carrier and a standalone chest rig for each weapon or application that you might have rather than putting together multiple plate carriers or simply swapping placards; I said "ostensibly" because ultimately they are still advocating that you buy more gear than you probably need. I also saw a rebuttal by First Line Defense Co. who argued to "just swap placards, you dummies" for which he got excoriated in the comments.

I think that this recommendation by Dirty Civilian, which by no means is new or an idea that they originated (and they admit as much), makes sense if you fashion yourself as a member of Tom Clancy's The Division; I don't think it makes sense for average civilians, which I will address later on in this post. But the other thing about the gear stacking recommendation is that it mostly adheres to the Unix Philosophy. The slick plate carrier and purpose-tailored chest rig are 1) individual things that do their individual jobs well (mostly, we'll get to this), 2) work together, 3) share a universal interface (your shoulders), and 4) are fairly easy to put together, test, and use. I don't think it's particularly elegant or cost-conscientious, and I can imagine it getting cumbersome with that amount of gear on your shoulders. I'm not sure what "self-supporting" would look like in this context so I think it's fair to ignore it.

So superficially, that's the Unix Philosophy in action. But I want to focus on one piece of that ecosystem: the chest rig. Regardless of whether you're using a micro rig, a "macro rig", a turnkey rig, or a rig you painstakingly put together using a variety of attachments (which is what I regrettably did), this is a tool that is doing more than just one thing. You could argue that the chest rig is an example of an ecosystem rather than one single tool, and that each component of that ecosystem is "doing one job and doing it well", which is valid. But I want to counter that with two questions:

* Are the things that it's doing things that you actually need?
* Is there an alternative that does the things you actually need even better, especially in a way that's elegant and conscientious of constraints?

If "mission drives the gear," I am skeptical that there exists a mission where a civilian is best served with an overbuilt, overequipped chest rig. They are slow to don, often carry more than you need for a realistic situation, and are designed with either military or milsimmers in mind. Anyone recommending these things or even plate carriers for home defense needs to have their gourd examined; it's a ridiculous suggestion and is a trasparent instance of "a purchase in search of a justification" or "gear in search of a mission".

#### The load-bearing equipment that does few things and does them well

To be clear: I actually do think there are legitimate use cases for body armor as far as civilians are concerned. The example where I have used my plate carrier most often is at the indoor gun range; shooters at public ranges are terrifying and have flagged me way more times than I want to think about, which was my initial reason for getting body armor. My other use case for body armor is the same use case for my gas mask: protest safety equipment. Even if the cops aren't wantonly shooting less-lethal rounds at protestors, there is the [legitimate risk][protest-shooter] that some Patriot Front failson will show up with an off-the-shelf AR that daddy gave them for Christmas and start mowing down First Amendment appreciators. 

I built a plate carrier around the protest use case. After initially getting a carrier with NIJ Level IV front, back, **and** side plates for range safety, it became apparent after wearing it for over 5 hours --even with aftermarket structural shoulder pads-- that it was painfully biting into my shoulder due to its weight, even after removing the side plates. I reconsidered my likely threats and ultimately replaced it with a better quality carrier and a pair of [special threat plates][rma-plates] that were significantly lighter. The carrier also has an IFAK that rides below the back plate, a boo-boo kit that rides below the front plate, and an elasticated cummerbund with First Spear tubes for better ergonomics and quick don/doff. It's not strictly slick, but I simply make sure not to wear any weapons-related gear on the carrier when I attend protests. In defense of First Line Defense Co.'s rebuttal, I do in fact "just run a placard, you dummy" for when this thing goes with me to the range, and that placard is really the only thing that swaps on and off. And to me, that is a better example of the load bearing equipmenet working as an ecosystem where each tool in that ecosystem is doing one thing and doing it well, and that each piece is modular such that if it's not needed for a given application, I can easily just remove it.

The other use case where civilians foresee needing a firearm and associated equipment is home defense. I already mentioned how it'd be wildly impractical to set up and expect to be able to use a plate carrier for a home invasion where you have minimal heads-up and even less time to react, so I have no delusions about my protest/range rig being shoehorned into that role. The only other alternatives that most folks are aware of are chest rigs, which we also already ruled out, and "battle belts". From my own testing, I find that having kit encircling my hips makes it much more difficult to work indoors. Additionally, I find that whether or not you should carry weight on your shoulders vs your hips is based on your own personal body mechanics, namely that you would probably be more comfortable carrying weight on whichever is wider: your hips or your shoulders; I have recurring hip pain, so my shoulders are relegated to mule duty. So then what can someone in my position with my arthritis- and neuroses-inspired set of criteria for load bearing equipment even use? The same tool that minutemen, partisans, and Wookiee smugglers of decades past had used to great effect: the bandolier.

I would say "this ain't your daddy's chest rig" but it quite possibly was your grandaddy's chest rig. The bandolier is a zero-fuss piece of load-bearing equipment that is as simple to put on as a sling (perhaps even simpler). No, it doesn't carry as much as a modern chest rig, and that's the whole fucking point. I've seen a [variety][invrt-review-1] of [reviews][invrt-review-2] for the IC13 INVRT bandolier specifically, and the recurring praise I hear for it is that it's quick to don, quick to draw magazines from, and forces you not to overload it with bullshit. That sounds exactly like what a civilian would want from a home defense rig, and every single reviewer I watched or read said exactly that. I am sold on this concept to the point that I intend to sell my two chest rigs to replace with one of these bandoliers. For home defense, I would set this thing up with at least one spare rifle magazine, a trauma kit (probably in an IC13 revolver pack or a Blue Force Gear mini IFAK; the IC13 trauma pouch looks cumbersome), a GMRS radio if I need to wander through the home to find pets and communicate updates to my partner, a handheld flashlight in a pistol mag pouch, and a bottle of pepper spray in another pistol mag pouch (because not all threats warrant lethal force).

## Train the way you fight, and compete the way you train

The cardinal sin I made in how I originally built out my load bearing equipment was that I sunk more money than I should have into building a rig specifically for competition. Yeah, my all M81 woodland camo chest rig that I built piece-by-piece looked cool as hell. But my sole use case for it was for hike-and-shoot events and wasn't suited to any likely practical scenario outside of that, so it just became an expensive pile of nylon that I exclusively wore to the range. I realize now that this was a backwards way to look at putting a load-bearing rig together.

If you're Daddy Warbucks and can build a completely new loadout for every competition you attend, this section doesn't apply to you. For the rest of us, particularly those that believe in the "beware the man with one gun" aphorism, competition is an opportunity to test equipment that we intend to use for practical applications; we want to see what works, eliminate what doesn't, and exercise our ability to use life-defending equipment under simulated stress so that we are more confident in our ability to perforum under real stress. Constantly changing equipment prevents us from developing the "muscle memory" that makes rote activities like changing a magazine completely second nature. So lock in on your practical loadout, and compete with that.

Another mantra that gets parroted in gun spaces is "train the way you fight" and unfortunately this is usually uttered by the same people trying to convince you that you need to equip yourself as if you are being air dropped into France and the Allies may or may not be coming to extract you. I said it before but I'll say it succinctly again: be honest about your use case, likely threats, and plausible scenarios where you might need a gun, and allow those needs to guide your ancilary gear choices. *THEN* you are ready to train the way you fight because you've realistically assessed how you will actually fight. And once you've got enough training with that locked-in set of equipment, compete the same way and with the same loadout that you trained with.

## TL;DR

* the Unix Philosophy emphasizes building a modular ecosystem where each piece "does one thing and does it well"
* this only works if you are actually building things that are practical, otherwise it's a waste of time, money, or effort
* be honest about your needs and use cases, and let that guide your kit-building plans
  - you probably won't need a chest right but might need a belt, bandolier, and/or plate carrier
* once you have honed in on a practical loadout, train with it as is and then compete with it as is
