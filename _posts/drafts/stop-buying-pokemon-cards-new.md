I've been on-and-off playing the Pokemon Trading Card Game since 1999. I have a particular fondness for the format that existed from 1999-2001 known as Base-Neo (as well as the later Rocket On format that tried to rectify some card power balancing issues endemic to the original Base Set run), and at that time both players and collectors existed in relative harmony: most of the cards that were considered collectible weren't competitively viable, and collectors largely focused on chasing and selling single cards rather than sealed product (of which there was just individual booster packs and theme decks). But then-publisher Wizards of the Coast saw the high demand and responded accordingly, printing the everloving hell out of the Base, Jungle, and Fossil sets and then later reprinting them as Base Set 2 and the Legendary Collection. You would have no difficulty walking into a card shop to find cards from this time period, even competitively viable staples, and often at a reasonable price.

After 2001, the popularity of the series began to wane; this coincided with Wizards of the Coast losing publishing rights to the TCG and The Pokemon Company taking over. Printing slowed, collecting died down, and coincidentally the game in terms of its design and mechanics entered its golden era from 2003-2007. Unfortunately, trying to acquire physical, legitimate cards from this era is not an inexpensive endeavor; the coparatively reduced printing run and resulting scarcity of these sets sees collectible cards (almost every card was competitively viable) commanding a premium price. A single copy of Holon Transceiver, a non-holographic trainer card that doesn't even feature any pokemon or character on it, typically costs $20 as of 2025.

The intervening years between 2007 and 2020 saw a variety of formats, steady growth of the game, a sea change in terms of the big names in the game (aka Jason Klazcinsky retired), and the new role of the social internet in deck testing, theorycrafting, match coverage, and drawing in new players. Once relegated to hobbyist message boards, the PTCG community now had YouTube and Twitch to expose the conpetitive circuit to a new generation and many more eyeballs. A game whose major tournaments once hardly filled a hotel conference room was now having to rent out bigger and bigger convention centers with each Regional tournament. 

And then the COVID-19 pandemic happened.
