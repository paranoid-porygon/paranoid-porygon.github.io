I got into a fairly heated argument online about whether or not simply building more housing in areas of the United States where there is high demand would drive down average home and rental prices. To my surprise, several self-described liberals not only argued that yes, more housing needs to be built, but also that this was all that was needed. They similarly argued that rent control measures were always bad (a talking point that I had not heard outside of [libertarian think tanks](article from macinac center)), that requiring that apartment complexes set aside a certain number of homes for people on housing assistance ended up inadvertantly harming young professionals and/or people (particularly queer people) trying to escape abusive homes, that there is no such thing as vacancy crises in high-demand housing markets, and that cartelization of housing markets is neither happening nor theoretically likely. Housing policy is nowhere near my area of expertise; the extent of my knowledge is a very basic understand of the economics at play and the conditions specific to my city and why our home and rental prices are so high, so I had to refresh my general knowledge of the economic research and meta-analyses on issues involving rent control and housing supply beyond just the anarcho-capitalist talking points I learned in my youth. I'm going to tackle each argument and topic I heard one at a time, but as a short and unsurprising summary: there is no simple, magic bullet fix to a housing crisis that was in the making for decades, and my findings reaffirmed my skepticism of anyone pretending that there is.

## Rent control and its discontents

The most commonly harped-upon point from that argument was whether or not rent control was useful or effective in any situation. The majority loudly argued that not only was it always countereffective but that there was universal consensus on this point by "several articles".

I looked up meta-analyses on studies about rent control, and while all of them highlighted the negative effects of rent control in the long term, the only one that took this kind of totalizing stance was from the [right-libertarian Macinac Center][macinac-article]. Every other article and summary by other think tanks took a much more measured stance; generally, they agreed that broad rent control was bad (which is why basically no city does this and instead codify numerous exemptions), that long term rent control has undesirable outcomes contrary to its goals, but that it also does and can have beneficial outcomes that the authors urged not be overlooked, specifically its ability to keep the poor housed.

## The poor vs. yuppies, abuse victims, and queer folks I guess?

This was a particulalry surprising argument that I heard where someone very clearly stated that attempts to keep rent low for the exceedingly poor ended up harming young professionals and people trying to escape their family homes for a variety of understandable reasons, and that those policies should be abandoned for that reason. The "just the facts" version of that statement sounds like this: "long-term rent control has the wider negative consequences of higher rents for everyone not in a rent-controlled unit, crowding out everyone except for those on housing assitance, lucky enough to get into and stay in a rent-controlled unit, and the exceedingly rich that can afford to live anywhere", which is true on its face but dances around the question of "so what can we do to ameliorate that *right now*?". I never got a direct answer, and the way this position was worded sounded more like "fuck the poors, long live the queer" chavinism than liberalism.

The facts that this position was based on are difficult to dispute. All the evidence confirms the undesirable effects of these policies used in the long term. What I take issue with is the idea that we necessarily need to pick favorites betwen the exceedingly poor, yuppies (young professionals), people escaping from abusive conditions, or the middle class more broadly, and that whatever vulnerable group we choose to prioritize should inform our policy preferences. 

If this is the game we're going to play where I'm forced to pick one to prioritize, my answer is going to be "the exceedingly poor"; we already take on the expense of funding a welfare state because we understand it to be a desirable function of the government that we take care of the extremely vulnerable, and requiring that apartment complexes earmark a certain number of units for those on housing assistance and capping the rents of those units is part of that project. This does lead to the increase of rents for all other units in that complex to offset the lost revenue, but the alternative is families being unhoused.

I'm not convinced that the externalities affecting the other groups at play are nearly as severe as they would be for the exceedingly poor. Yuppies would have to live at home a bit longer, something that adults have done for generations up until relatively recently without dire setback. Members of the middle class would have to make a choice between living near where they work or moving to the suburbs/exurbs; to be clear, this is not desirable and again would be **fixed in the long term by building more housing**, but is obviously a less bad outcome than being unhoused. The idea that vitcims of abuse trying to escape their households would be unable to due to homes being set aside for people on housing assitance struck me as a bit bizarre; unless victims of abuse are categorically ineligible for housing assistance, they aren't distinct from any of the other categories (the exceedingly poor, yuppies, or the middle class) in a way that meaningfully affects their membership among those categories and the economic conditions specific to them, except for instance if a yuppie who comes from an abusive or non-accepting family falls on hard economic conditions and cannot pay rent and cannot rely on their family to support them, which to be clear is not unique to just yuppies from abusive families (because some yuppies come from poor families, too). 

Ideally, the supply of homes will increase over the next 10 years and lead to a meaningful decrease in the price of homes and rentals such that housing assitance will not be needed as much, and that fewer people in dire need will lead to fewer rent-controlled units and their resulting detrimental market effects. But it is unrealistic to expect that nobody will ever need housing assitance, and simply supplying housing assitance without rent caps on homes eligible to be paid for with housing assitance will just cause the landlord to hike the rent to capture that allowance (a similar theory to [what happened with college expenses][bennett-hypothesis]).

## Vacancy crises

A quip that I heard was that "there are no vacancy crises in places where there is high demand to live", which might be true for most places inside the United States (for the moment) but is not by any means an ironclad rule of housing economics. The UK, Canada, and Australia all had to deal with excessive vacancies due to real estate speculation, a problem which still plagues Hong Kong who has been considering adapting the aforementioned countries' policies to address and even more severe crisis. 

This idea of "there is no vacancy crisis where people want to live" was joined by "luxury housing is just new housing and not a qualitatively different type of home", which again was not the case in markets like Hong Kong: the available homes that were sitting vacant were upscale apartments that speculators intended to rent to foreign businesspeople rather than allow to be rebuilt into reasonably priced apartments or condos. Whether or not this holds true in the US depends on the city and the particular market, but it's not by any means an impossibility that should be dismissed out of hand.

## Corollary arguments and analogies

Whenever there is a hurricane, states with gas pipelines likely to be affected institute emergency price caps on gasoline to avoid price gouging. Nobody thinks these are perfect solutions (in fact, rationing works much better, and both together are best) which is why these price caps aren't enacted in perpetuity and we instead let the market dictate the price during normal conditions, but price caps serve their purpose for the duration of the temporary emergency. The argument against any form of rent control or other policies responding to the housing shortage strikes me as denial that we are in an emergency (which we might not actually be in yet). 

## Is increasingly supply really all that it takes?

Everything that I read including basic intuition should be enough to persuade anyone that more housing needs to be built in high-demand parts of the United States. In most types markets, flooding with new supply should drive down prices, and even if you addressed everything else I discussed up to this point *except* supply, you still won't see prices go down without building new homes.

, namely that the zoning laws in my city prevent high-density construction of multi-family homes and the only homes being built are expensive townhomes, while rents have increased over the last 10 years at a rate that exceeds inflation, which would ultimlately be resolved *in the long term* by permitting more multi-family housing construction.
