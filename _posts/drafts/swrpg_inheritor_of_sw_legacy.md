While fans are not exactly starving for official Star Wars "content" at the moment, the broad consensus is that the Disney era has entered a slump; The Rise of Skywalker was the lowest-reviewed Star Wars film ever, new video games in the franchise have been widely panned, and average viewers for their live-action Disney+ shows have declined over time from show-to-show. This is not the first Star Wars media slump, however, and rather than responding by berating Lucasfilm employees on social media, fans should use it as an excuse to democratize (as much as you even *can* democratize a privately-owned franchise) storytelling in that universe like fans did back in the late '80s. Best case outcome is a repeat of the 1991-2012 golden era of Star Wars official and ancillary media; worst case is having to ignore whatever Disney produces and you tell your own stories using one of the existing Star Wars roleplaying games.

<!--excerpt-->

Between 1978 and 1991, die-hard Star Wars fans had little in terms of official ancillary media to enjoy between and after the film releases. As far as licensed published stories went, all that Gen X Star Wars fans had were the apocryphal Splinter of the Mind's Eye novel (1978), the Holiday Special (1978) which has since become lost media, the Ewoks (1984, 1985) and Droids (1985) children's cartoons, and some Marvel comics (1977-1987) featuring an anthropomorphic rabbit, all of which were of middling quality compared to the films themselves. 

Ultimately in 1987, tabletop game publisher West End Games was licensed by Lucasfilm to produce the original Star Wars Roleplaying Game, a move which, considering that no more official stories were being published under the brand, effectively handed Star Wars storytelling over to its fans. This decision would end up leading to the largest official expansion of the Star Wars mythos up to this point, the publication of the novel Heir to the Empire in 1991 which borrowed heavily from the game's sourcebooks, and rejuvenated popular interest in the franchise such that it convinced George Lucas to get back in the director's chair to create the prequel films.


In the years between the releases of Return of the Jedi and The Phantom Menace,  Interest certainly increased after the publication of Timothy Zahn's trilogy of novels, kickstarting what would become known as the Expanded Universe -- a series of books, comics, and video games that were ostensibly canonical entires in the series that were ancillary to the primary film saga -- and demonstrating to George Lucas that there was still enough interest in the series to justify getting back into the director's chair to create the prequels.

P.S. for the curious: I don't want to do a ranking of every Star Wars movie (because my ranking always changes, and I'm not sure doing that sort of thing makes sense to begin with), I will share my favorites from each trilogy:

Return of the Jedi
* the scene where Luke defeats vader, is told by the Emperor to kill his dad (the exact same advice that Obi-Wan and Yoda gave him), looks at his father's mechanical hand and then back at his own, and finally throws away his lightsaber is peak Star Wars.

The Phantom Menace
* despite the amount of consternation I direct towards Dave Filoni, I wll give him credit for highlighting what the prequels, and specifically TPM, are about: Anakin was in need of a father figure (Qui-Gon) but lost that and had to settle for being raised by his brother figure (Obi-Wan)
* the recurring motif of absent fathers in Star Wars plays out differently here than it did with Luke, who had both his Uncle Owen and Obi-Wan in addition to fabricated/mythologized stories about how his father was a successful spice trader / heroic Jedi Knight

The Last Jedi
* Kylo, unlike Luke and Anakin, actually had a revolving door of father figures (Han, Luke, Snoke), all of whom he rejects (and kills) and by the end of the film even repudiates the mythologized legacy of his grandfather, and as a result is left completely alone and directionless
* meanwhile Rey actively seeks parental figures, either biological or surrogate (Han, then Luke, and finally Leia) and is decpicted as both moral and capable in so doing
