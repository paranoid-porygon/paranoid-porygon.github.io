What if Google, Ground News, Palantir, Flock, Meta, and ABC/Disney all merged into a single company, and that company was then charged with facilitating all global elections, entertainment, and telecommunications? To any reasonable person in /[current year/], this idea sounds unambiguously dystopian and like something that everyone in the world, regardless of nationality or political affiliation, would agree should be rejected out of hand and actively frustrated if attempted. But that is precisely what the residents of the world of Infomocracy have signed up for. I would stipulate that Infomocracy ends up being another iteration of "Do Not Build The Torment Nexus: A Cautionary Tale", but most of the characters in the novel (and perhaps the author) adopt an ambivalent attitude toward this arrangement even by the end of the story. Except the main character/author self-insert. Maybe.

When I first discovered Infomocracy, it immediately shot to the top of my fiction TBR list. The premise of this novel ticks all the boxes of something I would be interested in and should love: post-cyberpunk, political thriller, femme protagonist written by a woman, speculation about alternative government and electoral systems. I made it my mission to hunt down physical copies of this entire series (Null States and State Tectonics are the sequels) and was fortunate to find all three at my local 2nd & Charles in very good condition for ~$6 a book. I had interacted with the author, Malka Older, on Twitter and BlueSky a few times and she struck me as friendly, very intelligent and knowledgeable about conflict and global emergencies, and a haver of good opinions; not nearly the big, sad doomer #Resistance lib her science fiction forebearers (William Gibson) act like on social media. All of that is to say, I was very excited to read this book and optimistic that it'd be the first of a new paradigm of post-cyberpunk --or cyberprep-- fiction.

I had set my expectations way too high for a debut novel written by an academic whose area of expertise wasn't creative writing, is what I later discovered. As a reader, various aspects of the prose, some half-baked world building, and an abrupt end that left some loose threads and tied up others with minimal sense of resolution left me wishing that a more experienced novelist had tried to tackle this novel's premise (I do have Neal Stephenson's Interface on my TBR, but Cryptonomicon was a massive turn-off so it's low on the list).

## Specific gripes

The prose, to be blunt, made me feel like I was reading a novel written for young adults.

The worldbuilding never takes the time to sit with its ideas and their implications. Abolishing historical boundaries and replacing them with population-contingent ones would surely have some huge cultural and identity-related consequences; why weren't these more fully explored? There is a lot of globe trotting (to the point that it almost felt like the author was trying to flex by name dropping places she had been to herself) but not much time was dedicated to exploring how these locales have changed since the new electoral system was implemented. Not much time is dedicated to explaining how the various different governments are different from each other; I can't tell you what major differences there were between Liberty and Heritage, who you would think would be different enough that they couldn't both be frontrunners if they were vying over the same voting base.

There is a lot of obvious self-insertion in Informocracy, which isn't intrinsically bad or by itself worthy of detraction. But some of it came with the obvious expectation that readers would silently nod and say, "yes, I understand and this is good and I need no further sales pitch as to why I should root for this." For instance, Policy1st feels like the author's dream party: an evidence-based policy shop that would appeal to scientists and self-styled intellectuals, but beyond that their guiding philosophy is unclear (are they utilitarian? liberal? social democratic? meritocratic? can you only hold office if you have a graduate degree? I have personally known academics who subscribed to a variety of ideologies, some more regressive and illiberal than others, while all claiming to priotize evidence-informed decision making).

## Less-specific, spoilery gipes

I had a bulleted list of plot complaints that I'm honestly a bit too far-removed from having finished this book to elaborate on, so I will leave them as bullet points:

* Liberty's purported aggression was hyped up throughout the book and then never carried through, abruptly defused
* hints were dropped that Information is behind everything but then that never gets fleshed out or fully investigated/exonerated
* Heritage's complicity in earthquake and election interference solved with minimal fanfare; very radid conclusion felt anti-climactic
* no explanation for how a new election was called, approved by voters, and smoothed over with widespread credibility
* minimal explanation about the Supermajority and what additional powers it confers to a government

## Final Word

I am told that the subsequent novels in this series see Older's writing and plotting improve, so I intend to read them and write an overall series review once finished. But Infomocracy does not stand on its own, and it brings me no pleasure to report as much.
