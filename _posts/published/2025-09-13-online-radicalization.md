I'm going to make my point up front in case you don't want to read everything that follows: if you are a parent of a child under the age of 24, especially young men or boys, it is of vital importance that you take an active role in minimizing your child's exposure to extremist and radicalizing content on the web if they are under 18, and to intervene if you detect extreme or antisocial behavior or ideologies if they are already legal adults. Your child is too young and does not have the maturity, knowledge, or moral framework for you to rely on them to be sufficiently critical of every single thing they encounter online, and are vulnerable to intentionally malicious influence. Tech, media, and gaming companies and platforms are overwhelmed with trying to keep up with the amount of malicious activity on their sites and apps and cannot be relied on to always protect your child. The buck ultimately stops with you, the parent, to model and if necessary police healthy social behaviors and internet usage.

Anything I say in this post comes fom either first-hand exploratory research that I've done professionally demonstrating how recommender algorithms feed illicit content to minors, second-hand from colleagues that work for social media platforms to counter extremism and crime, or from journalists, criminologists, and cybersecurity researchers reporting on this topic over the past decade.

# What is going on

In the last couple weeks, we have seen three different high-profile shootings carried out by young people in three different states. The information that we have about all three at this time indicate a shared pattern that has been common among almost all of these sorts of attacks over the past decade: these were isolated young people who spent most of their time in fringe online spaces that nurtured antisocial tendencies, eventually inspiring them to participate in [apolitical, nihilistic][1] acts of violence for the sake of [notoriety][2]. We can quibble about the role of guns or race or gender in these incidents, but the overwhelming pattern that presents itself across race, sex, gender identity, and geography of these assailants is that they were young and they were deeply ensconced in fringe communities on the deep web.

If you have not been active in niche online spaces over the past 15 years, it's difficult to succinctly explain all the memes, lingo, symbology, [subculture wars][3], groups, platforms, and personalities that are at play here, but understand that if your child spends a decent chunk of their time online, they have almost certainly been exposed to what I'm talking about. They exist in a whole other world that you may not be aware of, having conversations about topics that range from wild and ridiculous to unconscionable with total strangers. And some of what they say and do may be ironic, but more and more frequently we are seeing that young minds are increasingly "irony poisoned" where an edgy joke is taken too far into the real world because these are young minds that are not fully developed, still learning the skills of empathy and discernment, and have little to lose by acting out.

As children, they do not have the maturity, knowledge, or moral framework to navigate the unfiltered, uncurated internet. They do not possess the education or information context to properly discern truth from disinformation, propaganda, or intentional manipulation. Platforms like YouTube, Discord, Twitter, TikTok, or Instagram do not have the capacity to reliably filter all the content posted to their sites/apps to verify age appropriateness, let alone remove or provide warnings on content uploaded by bad actors. Parents cannot expect [tech companies][4] or their children to adequately sift through the garbage.

To put it in very simple terms: giving your kid a laptop or tablet with unsupervised and unfiltered internet access is like giving them keys to a sports car, a bottle of vodka, and a loaded gun.

Gone are the days when you can hand your child a Gameboy to keep them occupied for a while, knowing that they are safely playing offline by themself and not interacting with strangers, whackjobs, or predators they meet on the web. Very few personal portable computing devices these days are designed to work offline. Almost every application requires internet connectivity. There's a reason why the ESRB puts a asterisks next to the E or T ratings on video games that have an online multiplayer mode that says "online interactions not rated by the ESRB": the game developers don't control who your kid interacts with online, what they do there, or where they might be led to [outside of the game][5].

Kids' young brains are sponges, hungrily absorbing any info they find and following breadcrumb trails wherever they might lead; they are designed this way to enable their intellectual, social, and emotional development, and for this reason, it's not productive to take a punitive approach in guiding their online activities. It's better to model both healthy internet and screen usage as well as acceptable means of speaking and behaving, and to call out antisocial behavior in whatever form it takes, explaining why it's wrong from first principles.

# What you can do

In terms of what you can do from a technical perspective, I largely agree with [Jonathan Haidt][6] that it's not just unhealthy but unsafe to give children personal portable computers (laptops, phones, tablets) with uncurated internet access that they can use to access the internet (which is built by adult strangers and where they might interact with adult strangers) privately with no supervision; in the same way that you wouldn't let your child wander a strange new city unaccompanied, it's best to adopt the same mentality with the web. Instead, consider setting up a desktop computer for your child (or family) to use, and put it in a place in your home that's not the child's room. If you already got your child a portable personal computer, frame the switch to the desktop as an upgrade (in all honesty, it is; desktop computers generally have much better hardware and network speeds than portable devices).

I am not advocating that your child sacrifice all privacy to you or that you constantly hover over them as they browse the web or play games, but there should be the same level of oversight when they use the web as you would exercise when taking them to a playground.

If you are reasonably technical, I would setup the computer such that you the parent are the only one with access to the administrator account and that your child only has a user account without the ability to install applications. Any time that they want to install something, ask them:

* what does this do?
* where did you learn about it?
* are you able to interact with other users, and what do those interactions look like?

If any of their answers sound off, deflect with "Let me research it further before I install it," which you should probably do either way.

As far as game consoles: Nintendo tends to be much, much better about child safety than any other game console manufacturer or software developer. Most of their major franchises work perfectly fine without any internet connectivity, and the range of online activities or communication permitted on first-party (Mario, Zelda, Metroid, Splatoon) and second-party (Pokemon, Kirby, Smash Bros) games are realtively minimal and can reasonably be assumed to be safe. Third-party games (Minecraft, Roblox) are where online interactinos are less controlled and permit free-form text communication, and the available content is not curated or censored by Nintendo, so you the parent should plan to be present when they play those games, and ideally project them onto your television rather than in portable mode.

# Where do we go from here

Whatever your feelings on expanding gun control or access to mental health for children and young adults might be, nothing is being done on either of those fronts in any meanginful capacity that keeps pace with the rate of nihilistic youth violence. We have overwhelming evidence that most of these young people who engage in highly public violence are deeply isolated and extremely online, and that their internet usage and what they were doing and who they were communicating with was largely unknown to their parents. This is a fixable problem that cannot be easily offloaded to social media platforms, gaming companies, teachers, counselors, faith organizations, or the government. Parents are the people with the greatest --and quite possibly only-- ability to intervene.

I beg all the parents out there to stop using YouTube and Discord as a babysitter.
