# Preamble

I frequently hear remarks like "privacy is dead", "corporations/the government already have all our data", "there's no use in trying to be private anymore", and these remarks almost universally come from people who are either not particularly technically adept or are unwilling to put down the [distraction rectangle][distraction-rectangle] during their precious, limited free time on this Earth. My goal here is to solve both problems: to help inform you so that you may become more technically literate in service of protecting your personal privacy, and to hopefully convince you to spend less time on your phone. I also hope to disabuse readers of the myth that you can't claw back your privacy.

Privacy isn't dead, assuming you treat privacy as existing on a sliding scale as opposed to a binary exist/doesn't exist dichotomy. In fact, taking that binary position is called [borderline or dichotomous thinking][borderline-thinking] and can lead to actual clinical paranoia; if you only ever view yourself as "not private", you can end up in a spiral of deleterious behavior while you chase the unattainable condition of "completely private". But in the information security field, we understand that there is a balancing act to be done between usability and security, and that whichever you favor and what steps you can take to protect your information security is contingent on your "risk appetite" and threat model.

I used to dox people for a living. I worked as a digital investigator for a cybersecurity firm, and my job was to use OSINT (open source intelligence), WebINT (web intelligence), HUMINT (human intelligence), and social engineering to dig up personally identifying information on targets or directly engage with them and use psychological manipulation techniques to get them to reveal culpatory evidence. Even when targets thought that they were being sneaky or had redacted everything that might reveal their identities, often the sheer volume of posts they made was enough to give us something to go on and ultimately unmask them. And the scariest part about this was that the same techniques and tools that I used were just as accessible and easy to use by stalkers, maligned exes, disgruntled employees, or corrupt law enforcement. I say this not to instill paranoia but to demonstrate that I know how easy it is for private information to leak and also what steps you can take to plug those leaks; there were a few targets that I wasn't able to unmask, and that's because they took the precautions that I am going to suggest to you.

There are numerous guides online, most notably [Privacy Guides][privacy-guides], that offer a lot of info about what tools are available to users to augment their online privacy. I personally use many of their suggestions, however I plan for this guide to give specific recommendations that I have personally tested and can vouch for. Additionally, my guide will go beyond online and digital privacy and provide suggestions for offline security.

There is no magic bullet that will solve the problem of privacy in the online age. Simply paying for a VPN isn't going to plug every hole in your digital life (and in fact might [make you less private][vpn-scandals]). Privacy is a combination of a mindset, some best practices, and a few tools, all working in concert to plug as many hole as you can, a process often referred to as stacking of tolerances or simply *_stacking_*. Over time, these tactics will become second nature, and even if you aren't perfect about protecting your information, the fact that you do so _most of the time_ will go a long way to keeping your information, and subsequently *you*, safer. I'm going to share with you various elements of my *_"privacy stack"_* in the hopes that they might also be useful to you.

# Overview

Before you can start taking steps to maximize your personal privacy and data security, you need to make an honest assessment of the likely amount of risk you face. This assessment, known as your *threat model*, will inform where you are most vulnerable and what to lock down.

Once your threat model is sketched out, we will then talk about practical steps for maximizing your online privacy. The internet is a massive dumping ground of information, including data that you might have volunteered on social media, or that data brokers paid to collect on you from various public or semi-public sources, or that criminals have illicitly acquired from data breaches about you and then shared or sold on the dark web. We'll spend considerable time on this section.

We will also talk about offline privacy and security. Some of this will be about preventing data leaks during your daily offline life, but most of this section will be about steps you can take to fortify your physical safety.

We'll also talk about financial privacy and security as a separate section, as this is probably the most likely and significant threat faced by the average person.

## Key and useful terms

The three big terms that sometimes get mistakenly used interchangeably are _privacy_, _anonymity_, and _security_. These are related but distinct ideas that I want to differentiate before we go any further.

*Privacy* is the obscuring or hiding of sensitive information from people who you don't want to grant insight or access to. Closing the blinds or drapes on the windows of your home is an act of privacy; you aren't anonymous (your neighbors know who you are and that you live there), and the blinds don't secure your home (that's the job of the lock on your door), but people on the sidewalk can't see what you are up to.

*Anonymity* is the ability to carry out activities without having your identity associated with those activities. If I go to the store wearing a surgical mask, ballcap for my local team, and generic clothes and I pay with cash, I am acting anonymously. Related to anonymity is *pseudonymity*, which is roughly the same however you are sharing a false name or alias; your activities under that alias are still traceable back to the alias, but typically and ideally not traceable back to your real identity.

*Security* is the practice of denying access to people who shouldn't have access, granting acces to those who should have access, identifying when breaches in access occur, and responding accordingly to those breaches. Having a deadbolt on your door and only giving the key to members of your household is security; you might still have the blinds open and sacrifice your privacy, but nobody can get in the house.


# Threat Modeling

Someone who runs a small business that does e-commerce will need to advertise themselves in order to grow their business, so they are more risk tolerant than a teacher who doesn't want their facebook account to be found by their students and therefore adopts comparatively stricter privacy practices. That sort of thing is probably commonly understood, however I've seen people underestimate their relative risk because of an erroneous focus on corporate or government surveillance when their more likely threats are average people that they interact with on a day-to-day basis.

## Questions to help you develop your threat model

* Do you work a customer-facing job?
* Are you the sole proprietor for a small business?
* Are you a public servant, including but not limited to school teachers, law enforcement, journalists, clergy?
* Do you work a job that is the target of political rhetoric or activism, such as abortion providers, civil rights lawyers, media personalities, or politicians?
* Are you a woman or a member of a gender or sexual minority group?
* Are you a visible member of a religious community?
* Do you drive a nice or relatively new car?
* Do you have a disability or illness that can be exploited in order to harm you, such as epilepsy, a severe allergy or phobia, a heart condition, reliance on a medical device, or PTSD?
