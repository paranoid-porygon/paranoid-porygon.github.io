# Overview

I frequently hear remarks like "privacy is dead", "corporations/the government already have all our data", "there's no use in trying to be private anymore", and these remarks almost universally come from people who are either not particularly technically adept or are unwilling to put down the [distraction rectangle][distraction-rectangle] during their precious, limited free time on this Earth. My goal here is to solve both problems: to help inform you so that you may become more technically literate in service of protecting your personal privacy, and to hopefully convince you to spend less time on your phone.

Privacy isn't dead, assuming you view privacy on a sliding scale as opposed to a binary exist/doesn't exist dichotomy. In fact, taking that binary position is called [borderline thinking][borderline-thinking] and can lead to actual clinical paranoia; if you only ever view yourself as "not private", you can end up in a spiral of deleterious behavior while you chase the unattainable condition of "completely private". But in the information security field, we understand that there is a balancing act to be done between usability and security, and that which you favor and what steps you can take to protect your information security is contingent on your "risk appetite" and threat model.

Someone who runs a small business that does e-commerce will need to advertise themselves in order to grow their business, so they are more risk tolerant than a teacher who doesn't want their facebook account to be found by their students and therefore adopts comparatively stricter privacy practices. That sort of thing is probably commonly understood, however I've seen people underestimate their relative risk because of an erroneous focus on corporate or government surveillance when their more likely threats are average people that they interact with on a day-to-day basis.

I used to dox people for a living. I worked as a digital investigator for a cybersecurity firm, and my job was to use OSINT (open source intelligence), WebINT (web intelligence), HUMINT (human intelligence), and social engineering to dig up personally identifying information on targets or directly engage with them and use psychological manipulation techniques to get them to reveal culpatory evidence. Even when targets thought that they were being sneaky or had redacted everything that might reveal their identities, often the sheer volume of posts they made was enough to give us something to go on and ultimately unmask them. And the scariest part about this was that the same techniques and tools that I used were just as accessible and easy to use by stalkers, maligned exes, disgruntled employees, or corrupt law enforcement. I say this not to instill paranoia but to demonstrate that I know how easy it is for private information to leak and also what steps you can take to plug those leaks; there were a few targets that I wasn't able to unmask, and that's because they took the precautions that I am going to suggest to you.

There is no magic bullet that will solve the problem of privacy in the online age. Simply paying for a VPN isn't going to plug every hole in your digital life (and in fact might [make you less private][vpn-scandals]). Privacy is a combination of a mindset, some best practices, and a few tools, all working in concert to plug as many hole as you can. Over time, these tactics will become second nature, and even if you aren't perfect about protecting your information, the fact that you do so _most of the time_ will go a long way to keeping your information, and subsequently *you*, safer.
