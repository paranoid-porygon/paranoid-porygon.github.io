# Firearm Ownership for Self-Defense

## Safety, Recommendations, and Related Considerations

# Overview

I want to start by saying that I *do not* believe that everyone should own a gun. A cursory look at the statistics about firearm ownership shows that [more guns is correlated with more death](https://www.hsph.harvard.edu/hicrc/firearms-research/guns-and-death/), and a more nuanced dive into the available data shows where and why. Firearms are [the most common and most successful means of suicide](https://sprc.org/scope/means-suicide#:~:text=Firearms%20are%20the%20most%20common,followed%20by%20suffocation%20and%20poisoning.&text=Firearms%20are%20used%20in%20over%20half%20of%20all%20suicides%20among%20males.&text=In%202019%2C%20firearms%20were%20the,means%20of%20suicide%20for%20women.) in the US, affording the least opportunity for intervention, abortion, or survival than the other most common means. Hundreds of accidental firearm deaths occur every year, with [victims being primarily younger than 24 years old](https://www.aftermath.com/content/accidental-shooting-deaths-statistics/). In the absence of any laws or restrictions to mitigate the prevalence of firearm-related suicides or accidents, it falls upon the prospective firearm owner to sincerely and accurately [assess whether or not it is a good idea](#safe-storage) for them to purchase a gun, and if not to consider [alternate means](#non-lethal-options-and-preventative-measures) of home and personal defense. Anyone who tells you otherwise is likely trying to make money off you and doesn’t care about your safety.

If after honestly adjudicating whether or not you should get a firearm for self-defense you believe that you are safe to purchase one, this document outlines my recommendations as to [what firearm(s) you should purchase](#what-gun-should-i-get?), what [accessories](#accessories) you need to effectively utilize your weapon, what to look for in a [trainer](#training), and how to [practice](#practice) using your weapon.

Additionally, I cover other things you need to consider in deciding to own a firearm, such as [safe storage](#safe-storage) of the weapon, mandatory [first-aid training](#first-aid) (even if you decide against purchasing a firearm), minimizing [lead exposure](#lead-exposure) while shooting for practice or recreation, [licensure](#weapons-carry-permit), and [legal considerations](#the-law).

This document is by no means exhaustive or authoritative. I merely hope to provide initial information to help you start to navigate the world of firearms which is unfortunately infested with political hacks, brand zealots, swindlers, exploitative advertisers, dubious quality control, snake oil (sometimes literally), exaggerated credentials, hype-mongers, and outdated lore and myths. As with all things but especially with the realm of guns, you must adopt a skeptical attitude and not be shy about asking people to prove or demonstrate their claims, and be prepared to conduct exhaustive googling, forum/reddit browsing, and hands-on testing to find out what best fits your needs and performs as you expect.

# Safety

## Basic Firearm Safety {#basic-firearm-safety}

The most commonly known rules of firearm safety are the four rules codified by Col. Jeff Cooper:

1. Always treat a gun as if it’s loaded  
2. Keep your finger outside the trigger area until you are ready to fire  
3. Don’t point the gun at anything you aren’t willing to destroy  
4. Know your target and what’s beyond it

Many trainers will explain these and only these rules as if they are sufficient to keep you safe around guns. They are necessary and *must* be followed at all times when handling firearms, but I’d like to cover a couple more topics that have become more pertinent as guns have become more widespread and we have collected more data about firearm injuries and fatalities.

## Safe Storage {#safe-storage}

Hundreds of firearm deaths occur each year due to improper storage of arms and ammunition, with children as a common victim. Additionally, there are more annual incidents of firearm-related suicides than homicides in the US. 

Given those facts, you should consider the following before purchasing a firearm:

* Do I have the means to safely store a firearm? Am I ready to buy a gun safe at the same time as I buy my first firearm?  
* Do I have any children, roommates, or others in the home that I don’t want to have easy access to the firearm? Do I even let them know that I own a gun?  
* Do I or anyone else in the home have mental health issues or a history of suicidal ideation that might make owning a firearm an unsafe proposition? Do I buy a safe that I can access but keep the combination from the person in my family with a history of self-harmful tendencies? 

Self-preservation should be our \#1 concern when talking about firearms. We use guns for self-defense, so if owning a gun would increase your likelihood of hurting yourself, you may want to consider [non-lethal options](#non-lethal-options-and-preventative-measures) instead. That said, the mere seconds that it takes to get your gun out of a safe or lockbox could be enough for a suicidal episode to pass, to allow you to call for help, or for a family member to intervene.

### “Hold My Guns”

If you are moving to a new home, traveling abroad for an extended period of time, or have an unexpected mental health or other safety crisis, it’s beneficial to have a trusted friend or family member that can temporarily store your guns for you outside of your home. I recommend establishing this arrangement before purchasing your first firearm.

If you don’t have anyone nearby with the means to safely store your weapons, there is an organization known as [Hold My Guns](https://www.holdmyguns.org/) that has locations across the US that will store your firearms in situations of personal need.

## Safe Handling of Firearms

I can’t completely cover safe handling of firearms in this document. I would, however, recommend that you

1. Seek out a gun store or friend/family member that can help you try various firearms that they recommend for your specific needs while teaching your the basics of firearm handling  
2. Make preparations to safely store the firearm you intend to purchase  
3. Purchase the firearm you decide on, as well as a holster (specific to the gun) or sling depending on if it’s a pistol or rifle, respectively  
4. Attend a basic pistol or rifle class to attend with your new firearm before you carry it or keep it staged for use  
5. Take [a first-aid course](#first-aid), at minimum a [Stop The Bleed](https://www.stopthebleed.org/) course (offered at several gun ranges, fire departments, or even online) and ideally a [Red Cross CPR/AED](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/cpr) course as well

Additionally, never handle firearms while under the influence of intoxicants, especially illegal drugs. Drinking and shooting is immensely and obviously dangerous, but owning a firearm while in possession of a federally prohibited substance (including marijuana, regardless of its legality in your state) is a federal crime and can get you prison time in addition to being banned from ever owning a firearm ever again.

Even if you have years of experience with firearms, it doesn’t hurt to take occasional classes to learn to use yours better, reinforce shooting fundamentals, or identify your weapon’s limitations or failure points.

### Lead Exposure {#lead-exposure}

Despite its well-known association with cancer, developmental disorders, intellectual and psychological diseases, and even erectile dysfunction, many gun owners are nonchalant regarding their heightened exposure to lead and other heavy metals. This is a dangerous attitude to adopt. 

Lead poisoning is cumulative: the lead that gets into your body adds up over time and doesn't quickly disappear, if at all. Once you've got it, it's there likely forever, and you shouldn't go to the gun range anymore. Children and pets are even more susceptible to it.

But lead poisoning is preventable. When using an indoor gun range, wearing a [respirator with filters rated for lead](https://www.pewpewtactical.com/best-gas-masks-respirators-filters/) virtually eliminates any meaningful exposure to lead while shooting. After shooting, regardless of venue, D-Lead makes [body soaps](https://esca-tech.com/product/d-lead-deluxe-whole-body-wash-and-shampoo/) and [detergents](https://esca-tech.com/product/d-lead-respirator-laundry-detergent/) that you should use that remove heavy metals including lead from your skin, hair, and clothes. Remember to immediately dump your clothes in the wash, remove and put your shoes where animals and children can't get to them, and take a shower as soon as you get home from a range trip.

# Recommendations

## What gun should I get? {#what-gun-should-i-get?}

Assuming you followed my above recommendations to assess if you are safe to own and prepared to safely store and handle a firearm, let’s talk about what gun to get. The simplest answer to this question is: “**a Glock 19**”. The more complicated answer is that it depends on your needs, your hand size and shape, and how much time you are willing to dedicate to training and practice.

### Should I get a shotgun?

**No**. 

Despite what you might hear from hunters, Fudds (old-fashioned gun owners that resemble Elmer Fudd), or former and [current Presidents of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buy_a_Shotgun#:~:text=Biden%20noted%20that%20he%20personally,difficult%20to%20aim%20and%20use.), shotguns are terrible for home defense. They overpenetrate drywall, increasing the risk of destroying not just your home but harming other members of your family or even your neighbors if you have to shoot it in the house. They are deafeningly loud. They have significantly more recoil, less ammo capacity, usually require manual action to cycle, and often have terrible sights, making it significantly more difficult to make followup shots if you don’t take out the threat with your first one (usually the case) or if there are multiple threats (statistically likely). They *do* actually require you to aim, as their cone of fire is smaller at close ranges than Fudds would lead you to believe.

Shotguns were designed to hunt animals outdoors. Pistols and rifles were designed to kill humans indoors and out.

### Should I get a pistol?

Almost always, the answer to this question is “yes”. **A pistol is a good option for home defense, and the only option for personal defense.** They are concealable (provided you have a [permit](#weapons-carry-permit) or are on your own property), can be operated with one hand (leaving your other hand available to open doors, hold a flashlight, or call 911), and fire lower velocity projectiles that are less likely to over-penetrate a target or drywall.

The only drawbacks to a pistol are that they are harder to aim and place followup shots with than a weapon with a buttstock (but regular practice makes this a non-issue) and that they are more likely to be discharged accidentally than a long gun. If you are not confident that you will have the time or finances to regularly practice with a pistol or do not intend to carry a concealed weapon, you may want to consider getting a rifle or carbine instead as they are easier to use.

#### Revolver or Semi-automatic?

There’s a misconception that revolvers are more “reliable” and therefore better options when your life depends on your gun. This definition of “reliability” is based on the weapon’s likelihood to malfunction which, while true that a semi-automatic pistol is more likely to have a malfunction than a revolver, doesn’t tell the whole story. 

First, the overwhelming majority of semi-auto malfunctions can be remedied with the “tap-rack-bang” method: tap the bottom of the magazine to ensure that it is seated properly, rack the slide to eject the stuck casing or misfired round, and then go back to pulling the trigger to make it go bang; this remedies the malfunction in under a second. 

Second, revolvers *do* in fact malfunction, and when they do, it is catastrophic and cannot be repaired without tools.

Third, the revolver has so many other drawbacks (heavy trigger, lower ammo capacity, slower reload, generally harsher recoil, worse ergonomics, terrible sights) that these problems easily outweigh its perceived benefits over semi-auto pistols.

So if we redefine “reliable” to mean “overall likelihood of serving me well when my life depends on it” rather than simply “likelihood of not having a minor malfunction”, **semi-automatic pistols are vastly more reliable than revolvers**.

That said, one major advantage of some revolvers is that they are very small and very concealable, specifically J-Frame style revolvers. If you need what we call “deep concealment”, a J-Frame revolver is a good choice. But generally, a semi-automatic pistol will serve you better in almost every scenario.

#### Striker or Hammer?

Semi-automatic pistols usually work in one of two ways: a (usually visible) hammer falls when you pull the trigger to strike the firing pin to fire a round, or an internal spring-loaded metal “striker” is released and directly strikes the round when you pull the trigger. These matter because they bring with them different levels of safety and ease of use.

To be clear: well-made modern pistols, whether striker or hammer, are overwhelmingly safe, provided they are used properly. That said, there is a heightened risk of improper use with a hammer pistol than a striker pistol; if the hammer hits the firing pin when you don’t plan on it (if you limp-thumb the hammer when cocking it, if you drop the gun with the hammer down, etc.), the gun might go off. These types of accidents are less likely or impossible with a striker-fired pistol simply due to how it is designed.

Striker-fired pistols tend to have less heavy triggers and are therefore easier to use. There are caveats to this, however. Single-action hammer pistols can be carried with the hammer cocked, but that then requires keeping the safety engaged, which is another thing to think about if you have to draw and use your weapon. Double-action and double-action/single-action hammer pistols can be carried with the safety off (not recommended), but this is usually done with the hammer down which leaves you with a very heavy trigger. 

**Striker-fired pistols come with virtually none of these concerns** and a moderately weighted trigger.

#### Full, compact, or subcompact size?

Most manufacturers make their handguns in at least three sizes: full-size, compact, and subcompact. 

Full-size is a “duty” pistol usually designed to be carried openly, particularly by military or law enforcement. They tend to have around 5-inch barrels as well as grips that people with larger hands can comfortably hold. These can be carried concealed but usually only with a heavier winter coat, otherwise the imprint they make on your clothing will be obvious. They make a good home defense option.

Compact is the middle ground option for handgun sizes. They have both a smaller frame and barrel than full-size pistols, and that comes at only a negligible difference in ammo capacity (15-rounds vs 17-rounds when talking about stock 9mm magazines). They are more concealable while still being reasonably controllable in terms of recoil and grip real estate.

Subcompact is the most concealable of the main pistol size offerings. Sometimes called “pocket pistols” because some are so small that they can fit in a pocket holster, that small size and concealability comes at a price: they have significantly less ammo capacity, are harder to grip for almost every hand size, have snappier recoil, and are harder to aim accurately. That said, as  mentioned before when talking about revolvers, they may be your only option in situations that call for deep concealment, and aiming at a human-sized target at very close distances isn’t difficult even with a subcompact gun.

If you only plan to own one pistol, a **compact size handgun provides the best all-around performance** in terms of controllability, concealability, and ammo capacity.

#### What caliber?

**9mm**.

That may sound terse seeing as there are valid cases to be made for different calibers in different situations, namely based on ballistic performance. But for home and personal defense, the ballistics of 9mm are sufficient. Additionally, the size and weight of 9mm allow you to carry more in a single magazine than .40 or .45 (the other most popular calibers for semi-auto pistols) while retaining similar ability to subdue a target. 9mm is also usually cheaper than other defensive calibers.

As for projectile weight and type for 9mm, 124gr hollow point or 147gr hollow point are good for personal and home defense, and 115gr FMJ is good for practice, target shooting, and competition.

If for whatever reason you forego 9mm, I advise against going any smaller in terms of bullet diameter than 9mm when selecting an alternative cartridge as smaller cartridges have been known to be insufficient to subdue targets. Also consider the velocity and penetrative ability of whatever cartridge you select, as you do not want to overpenetrate a wall or target and hit a bystander or neighbor.

#### Why don’t some pistols have safeties?

People unfamiliar with modern firearms generally expect every gun to have a manual “safety”, also known as a safety selector, and are surprised to learn that a lot of striker-fired pistols do not. This is largely due to design differences between older style firearms, like hammer pistols, that need safeties to ensure that the hammer doesn’t accidentally fall and the newer striker pistols that don’t have hammers and therefore don’t need manual safety selectors, instead relying on “internal safeties” or “trigger safeties” to guard against the gun going off if it is dropped.

While some people prefer guns with manual safety selectors because they afford positive, visual confirmation that a gun is not ready to fire, I offer two counter arguments to that school of thought.

First, relying on a safety selector to confirm that a gun is safe violates the [basic rules of gun safety](#basic-firearm-safety). A gun isn’t safe just because a little switch says it is; that little switch can fail or be wrong. A gun is safe if you treat it like it’s loaded, don’t point it at anything you aren’t willing to destroy, isn’t going to hit anything beyond your target that you don’t want it to, and doesn’t have your finger inside the trigger area. The safety selector is redundant and sometimes even deceptive.

Second, having a safety selector on a gun that you plan to carry concealed in a holster that is already protecting the trigger from being accidentally pulled adds an unnecessary step to your draw. On top of having to safely draw, grip, point, aim, and fire, a safety selector makes it so that you now also have to flip a tiny switch using fine motor skills while under duress. In a self-defense scenario, you want to have to use as few fine motor skills as possible, but choosing to carry a pistol with a safety selector unnecessarily introduces a need to use a fine motor skill or your gun simply won’t work.

If your gun at rest isn’t safe without a manually engaged safety selector, I argue that said gun isn’t good for self defense purposes. For that reason, **I once again recommend striker-fired pistols with trigger/internal safety mechanisms** over hammer pistols.

All that said, there are occasionally defects or flaws in certain pistol designs, even [striker-fired pistols](https://www.wcvb.com/article/sig-sauer-hit-with-new-lawsuit-5-investigates/42159156), where a gun that is otherwise safely stored or holstered might go off if accidentally dropped. Handguns usually go through extensive “drop safe” testing before being brought to market, but unfortunately consumers are more numerous than quality control testers so issues might not be identified until after going up for sale. For this reason, make sure to check if there are reports of catastrophic safety issues or recalls on a handgun you intend to purchase. **A good rule of thumb is to purchase a gun from a reputable manufacturer and of a model that has been around for a while** rather than whatever the new hotness is.

#### Are you telling me I should just get a Glock 19?

Yes, I am. It’s a semi-automatic striker-fired compact 9mm pistol from a reputable manufacturer that has been used in combat and self-defense for decades, and has the largest availability of aftermarket accessories (including holsters, lights, and optics) and replacement parts of any firearm in history. It is the most used pistol in the world for a reason.

At the very least, **try a Glock 19 and then, based on what you like and what you don’t, try other guns that fix the specific things you don’t like about the Glock 19**.

Too small? Try a Glock 17\.  
Too big? Try a Glock 26\.  
Don’t like the sights? There’s a ton of aftermarket options for the Glock, including optics.  
Want a manual safety? Try a Beretta Px4 Compact.  
Want a snub-nosed revolver for deep concealment? Try a Ruger LCR.

All that said, your use cases or restrictions might not lend themselves to getting a pistol at all, in which case I recommend getting a rifle or carbine.

### Should I get a rifle?

**Probably\!** Rifles are easier to handle, less often involved in accidents, and usually subject to fewer restrictions than handguns. There’s also an argument to be made that long guns are [less likely to be used in suicide attempts](https://injepijournal.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s40621-019-0230-y) than handguns, though that statement comes with several caveats. 

The downsides to a rifle come down to its size and its ammunition. A rifle can’t be concealed, so it isn't ideal to keep on your person when you get an unexpected knock on your door after dark. Depending on barrel length, it might be more difficult to maneuver indoors with a rifle. With some exceptions, rifles usually can’t be operated one-handed as well as they can with both hands, making it difficult to be on the phone with 911 or carry a child or pet while holding the weapon. And some calibers and ammunition types aren’t ideal for defensive use because of their propensity to overpenetrate.

If your finances allow, and assuming you don’t live in an apartment, I recommend having both a rifle and a pistol. The pistol is for regular concealed carry or situations where a confrontation isn’t guaranteed (an unexpected knock on the door and you keep it concealed on your hip), and the rifle for when a confrontation is already in progress (home invasion).

#### Caliber

The answer to this isn’t as clear-cut as it was for handguns because the caliber you want for your rifle is dependent on the use case for your rifle, and the use case for your rifle is dependent on your environment and likely threats. Do you live in an apartment/duplex/townhome/condominium, a single family home in a neighborhood, or in a rural area with no houses nearby? Are your likely threats going to be two-legged, four-legged, or possibly either?

The other thing to consider is that each caliber performs differently depending on weight of the projectile (or grains) and length of the rifle barrel (which will be discussed in the next section).

I’ll briefly discuss some common calibers that I think are worth considering and scenarios where they might be the best choice. 

##### 5.56x45mm NATO

The 5.56 (pronounced five-five-six) is the standard cartridge for the AR-15 and for NATO nations’ militaries. It is a small-caliber high-velocity (SCHV) cartridge that gets its ballistic performance from its speed. Depending on the weight (grain) of the bullet and length of the barrel, 5.56 can have impressive penetrative capabilities and a useable range of around 500 meters.

To be completely clear: 5.56 is a military cartridge that is designed to kill humans from a distance, and it even has the ability to piece body armor under the right conditions.

##### .223 Remington

The most common rifle cartridge in the United States, .223 (pronounced two-two-three) was a civilian cartridge that eventually evolved into the 5.56 NATO. Because of that, any rifle chambered in 5.56 can also shoot .223, **but not the other way around**. This is because 5.56 rifles are designed to handle the higher pressures of the 5.56 round and can easily handle .223, but those higher pressures would catastrophically damage and possibly injure the user of a .223 rifle who erroneously loaded it with 5.56 ammo.

The .223 cartridge is also a small-caliber high-velocity projectile

### Pistol Caliber Carbines

Luckily, there is a middle ground option between a rifle and a pistol: the pistol-caliber carbine (PCC). A carbine is usually understood to be a rifle with a barrel length of less than 20 inches. And as the name implies, a PCC has a barrel shorter than 20 inches and shoots a pistol round instead of a rifle round, providing you with the ballistic advantages of a pistol with the handling characteristics of a rifle.

## Gun Safes and Lockboxes

You should purchase a safe either before or at the same time as you purchase your first firearm. It does not need to be fancy: any hard-sided case that your gun fits in with a locking mechanism will work fine. The purpose of the safe is to prevent unauthorized access, either by thieves, children, or folks who shouldn't handle firearms for one reason or another.

An often overlooked advantage to keeping your gun in a safe is that if you, the gun owner, have a mental health episode that leads to suicidal ideation, the extra time needed to fetch the gun from the safe might be enough for the episode to pass, someone else to intervene, or allow a moment to reach out for help.

Either of these is reason enough to always keep your gun in a safe. As for whether or not to store your weapons in the same place as your ammo, that is up to your state laws and your discretion. I personally keep my carry gun loaded, a round chambered, and in a holster while in the safe and my home defense carbine loaded, chambered, and on safe. All other guns I keep unloaded and ammo separate.

# Considerations

## Weapons Carry Permit {#weapons-carry-permit}

### What about open carry?

Open carry is a very bad idea. You end up unnecessarily intimidating and putting on edge people around you (including and especially police or other people that might be packing). You make yourself a priority target if you happen to find yourself in the middle of a robbery, mugging, or shooting spree. Depending on where you have your holster, you may end up becoming a gun dispenser for someone that gets the drop on you.

Do not carry openly within city limits. Save that for the gun range or backwoods hiking.

## Non-lethal Options and Preventative Measures {#non-lethal-options-and-preventative-measures}

### Threat Assessment and Avoidance

### Pepper Spray

There are certain venues where you might not be able to or want to carry a firearm: religious gatherings, school, anywhere that you might physically interact with children, clients, or customers, formal events, parties, places where you might be consuming alcohol. You may even find yourself in a situation that calls for self-defense but you aren’t sure or don’t think lethal force is justified, or that a firearm is safe to use: an altercation with a minor, a vague threat that hasn’t yet escalated to demonstrable assault, a vicious dog is coming at you and you aren’t sure you can shoot a small and fast target, you fear injuring a bystander but can’t readily escape.

These situations are where pepper spray is a viable option. Anyone can carry pepper spray and doing so does not require a permit. It is relatively inexpensive, usually less than $20. It is extremely concealable. It is easier and safer to use than a stun gun (which I would categorize as semi-lethal or less-lethal, whereas pepper spray is non-lethal). You can have pepper spray in almost any location with the exception of the airport. It confers minimal risk of self-injury, and even then the level of possible injury is less than a firearm or stun gun.

Even if you decide against getting a firearm, you should carry pepper spray.

### Outdoor Cameras and Signs

### A Watchdog

You don’t need to have a police-trained Doberman Pinscher to protect your home. Any sufficiently alert and vocal dog makes a good watchdog. The barking will alert you that someone is near the house, and often the barking by itself is enough to ward off would-be intruders. 

Anecdotally, I have found sheepdogs (shelties and aussies in particular) to be the best watchdogs while still making incredibly loyal, trainable, and loving pets. I emphasize that you *must train your sheepdog or suffer the consequences of a bored, smart dog that doesn’t listen to you.*

### What about MMA, Jiu Jitsu, Krav Maga, \[insert martial art du jour\]?

Speaking with folks that do competitive martial arts, their consensus is that in any street fight, the more athletic person almost always wins. It requires an immense amount of time, dedication, athletic and physical ability, and genetic/health predisposition to get proficient at martial arts, and even then you still run the risk of getting seriously injured in a street fight even if you win. Anyone who says otherwise is, once again, likely trying to sell you something.

You are better served by avoiding confrontation, deescalating if you can, and using tools that let you maintain distance and end the fight quickly, be they lethal or non-lethal. You’re not looking for a fight, let alone a fair one; you’re looking to survive, by any means necessary. 

## Physical Fitness

The benefits of regular exercise and a reasonable diet are well-known and don’t need to be fully rehashed here. That said, one area of fitness that I want to highlight and recommend to focus on is the ability to sprint.

If you do no other exercise, and provided you are physically able, making sure you are able to run fast for short distances will serve you well in a self-defense scenario. Your goal in a self-defensive situation is to survive, and that means being able to flee, run to cover, or outrun your assailant.

Also take your vitamins.
